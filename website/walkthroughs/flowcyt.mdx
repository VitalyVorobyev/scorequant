# Bone-marrow cell populations

## The cells, their labels, and a patient's fractions

The data is the FlowCyt classification benchmark: bone-marrow aspirates measured by flow
cytometry, one cell at a time. For every cell the instrument records a forward-scatter and a
side-scatter intensity and one fluorescence intensity per marker channel, twelve numbers in all,
and an expert has labelled each cell as one of six populations: T cells, B cells, monocytes, mast
cells, the CD34-positive progenitors (HSPCs), and everything else. The quantity to estimate for a
patient is the composition, the fraction of that patient's cells in each population.

This page asks what happens when a patient is summarised by a few integer counts instead of the
cells themselves: each cell is assigned to one of a small number of categories, and the population
fractions are then fitted from the category counts. Which categories should they be, and how much
of the composition do they cost? The categories here are chosen from the estimation problem and
frozen before the held-out patients are seen.

<CompositionBars patients={exploration.patients} populations={dataset.populations} />

Each bar above is one patient's composition, and the spread between them is the thing being
measured. It is much wider than the error any of the methods below make, which is what keeps the
small numbers later on meaningful rather than merely small.

## The data and its licence

The cohort was split once, before anything was fitted, into a reference group that the
compression is built from and a held-out group that nothing downstream has seen. Every number on
this page that is called held out was computed on that second group.

## From marker channels to a score

The score model here is Door 3, the density-ratio route. A patient-cross-fitted classifier
estimates each cell's posterior over the populations under the mixture it was trained on. If those
posteriors are calibrated, dividing each by its population's training proportion gives that
population's density relative to the training mixture, and the ratio of any two of them is the
posterior odds over the prior odds, which is the ratio of the two population densities. The
mixture parameterization turns those ratios into a score — the derivative of a cell's log
likelihood with respect to the population fractions. One population is absorbed as the simplex-dependent
reference, so the score has one coordinate per remaining population:

Two of the boxes in that chain are deliberately not ScoreQuant. The classifier is not, and the
mixture likelihood that finally turns counts into fractions is not:

By the time ScoreQuant is called, that map has already been applied, so what the library receives
is a table of score rows and their weights — precomputed scores, plus a declaration of where they
came from. A source and a score provider are separate contracts, and the library will not let the
two be confused: a table already in score space refuses a provider, and a table of raw
observations insists on one, because without the observation-to-score map there is no score law to
optimise against.

```python
flowcyt = sq.ScoreSample(
    scores,
    weights,
    provenance=sq.ScoreProvenance(
        kind="estimated_ratio",
        description="patient-cross-fitted calibrated FlowCyt mixture score",
    ),
)
assert flowcyt.provenance.exact_fisher is False

try:
    sq.fit_quantizer(flowcyt, provider=sq.ScoreFunction(lambda x: np.asarray(x)), n_bins=8)
    raise AssertionError("a ScoreSample must reject a provider")
except ValueError as error:
    score_sample_rejects_provider = str(error)

try:
    sq.fit_quantizer(sq.ObservationSample(scores), n_bins=8)
    raise AssertionError("an ObservationSample must require a provider")
except ValueError as error:
    observation_sample_requires_provider = str(error)
```

The provenance is not decoration either. `kind="estimated_ratio"` is a statement that a classifier
produced these numbers, and `exact_fisher` is derived from it rather than accepted as a flag, so
nothing downstream can describe this fit in exact-Fisher language. The last section of this page is
about what that costs.

## The fit

The call is the ordinary one. It takes the source, a bin budget, a criterion, and the
configuration for the solver that criterion selects:

```python
soft = sq.fit_quantizer(
    flowcyt,
    n_bins=8,
    criterion=sq.DOptimality(),
    config=sq.SoftVoronoiConfig(seed=2026, initializer_restarts=4, max_steps=160),
)
exchange = sq.fit_quantizer(
    flowcyt,
    n_bins=8,
    criterion=sq.DOptimality(),
    config=sq.DExchangeConfig(seed=2026),
)
assert soft.n_bins == exchange.n_bins == 8
assert soft.information_kind == exchange.information_kind == "supplied_score_surrogate"
```

`DOptimality` is the criterion that treats every population fraction as equally worth keeping: it
maximises the determinant of the information the labels carry, which is the same as minimising the
volume of the joint uncertainty ellipsoid over all the fractions at once. The alternative — naming
one fraction as the parameter of interest and letting the rest float — is a different inferential
question, and the last section reports what it bought here.

The two configurations are two solvers for that one criterion, and this study runs both. The soft
Voronoi fit relaxes the hard assignment into a temperature-annealed soft one, optimises the
centres with Adam, and hardens at the end; `initializer_restarts` is how many k-means starts seed
it and `max_steps` how many Adam updates it takes, both set to the study's values, and `seed` is
what makes the whole thing reproducible. The exchange fit instead moves single rows between cells
while the exact determinant improves, and stops when no single move helps. Neither is judged on
its soft objective: both are scored after hardening, on patients neither of them has seen.

The two solvers disagree, and they disagree in opposite directions on the two metrics: soft
Voronoi wins on estimation error, the exchange wins on retained information. That is not a
contradiction — the downstream fit is a fixed-total multinomial likelihood on the counts, not the
local Fisher quadratic the criterion optimises — but it does mean any sentence about this study
has to say which method produced the number in it.

A validation source can be attached to any fit, and it is diagnostic only: it never influences a
gradient, a stopping rule, an initialization choice, or a checkpoint. Here the committed table is
split by alternating rows, which keeps every population on both sides:

```python
fitted = sq.fit_quantizer(
    sq.ScoreSample(scores[::2], weights[::2]),
    validation=sq.ScoreSample(scores[1::2], weights[1::2]),
    n_bins=8,
    criterion=sq.NormalizedTrace(),
    config=sq.KMeansConfig(seed=4, solver_restarts=4),
)
train_efficiency = float(fitted.train_report.geometric_mean_retention)
holdout_efficiency = float(fitted.validation_report.geometric_mean_retention)
assert 0.0 < holdout_efficiency <= 1.0
```

That is the mechanism; the study proper holds out whole patients frozen before any fitting rather
than alternate rows of one table. Its solver comparison, score calibration and per-patient
estimates are in the{" "}

## What that means for the composition

On this cohort a patient can be reduced to {f("bins")} integer counts and still yield the
population fractions to within an error far smaller than the differences between patients. The
categories are not rectangles on a two-channel projection; they are chosen from the estimation
problem itself, and they are frozen, so the same rule applies to every patient in the cohort and
to the next one that arrives. How many categories that takes is measured below rather than
assumed.

## What simpler reductions retain on the same cells

way: fit the fractions from the counts on the frozen patients, and measure the result against the
expert composition.

The three baselines use the same cells and the same budget without the score geometry. A
rectangular grid on two marker channels is a two-dimensional gate with straight edges. Clustering
the raw marker vectors groups cells that look alike and counts the groups. Cutting a single score
direction into intervals keeps the classifier output but summarises it in one dimension. All three
land an order of magnitude away on estimation error as well:

The gap widens rather than closes as bins are added, which is the useful part: a grid or a single
direction cannot spend extra categories usefully, because the directions it is spending them on
are not the ones carrying the answer.

## What this does not do

It does not improve the measurement. The unbinned classifier ratio, with no compression at all,
reaches {f("unbinnedMacroRmse")} macro RMSE; soft Voronoi, the better of the two methods on that
metric, reaches {f("softVoronoiRmse")}. Compression costs, as it must: the information carried by
a label is never more than the information carried by the thing it replaced. The finding worth
reporting is the size of the cost: those two errors are of the same order, and both
sit far below the spread between patients in the first chart, so replacing the continuous ratio
with {f("bins")} integer counts does not change what the report says. If a continuous ratio is
available to you at analysis time, use it; this page is for when it is not, because the summary
has to be archivable, transmissible, or auditable.

The second limit is in the provenance. The classifier's ratios are estimated, so the result is
recorded as <code>{f("informationKind")}</code> and not as exact Fisher information. What the reported
D-efficiency measures is how much of the *estimated* score the bins keep — a number that stays
high whether or not the estimate itself is any good.{" "}
<a href="/walkthroughs/ratios">From a classifier to Fisher-preserving bins</a> measures the gap between
those two things on a problem where the exact score is known, and it is the honest companion to
this page.

The third is a negative result the study went looking for. Aiming the objective at one compartment
— the HSPC fraction, with the rest of the composition floating — barely moves anything: on the
committed fixture, the plain D partition already retains {f("profiledDPartitionRetention")} of the
profiled information against a certified ceiling of {f("profiledCeilingRetention")}. A criterion
aimed at one fraction is a different inferential objective, not an automatically better one, and
here there was almost nothing left for it to win.

## The same door, other inputs

Precomputed scores are not only for classifier output. When a model is analytic, the score is
known in closed form and the same table can be declared exact — and then, and only then, the
result is allowed to report exact Fisher information. The scores of a Gaussian location model at
the origin are the observations themselves, which makes the smallest possible honest example:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
normal_scores = rng.normal(size=(2_000, 2))
normal_weights = np.ones(normal_scores.shape[0])

analytic = sq.ScoreSample(
    normal_scores,
    normal_weights,
    provenance=sq.ScoreProvenance(kind="exact", reference_point=(0.0, 0.0)),
)
assert analytic.provenance.exact_fisher is True

rule = sq.fit_quantizer(
    analytic, n_bins=6, criterion=sq.DOptimality(), config=sq.DExchangeConfig(seed=7)
)
assignment = sq.optimize_partition(
    normal_scores, weights=normal_weights, n_bins=6, config=sq.DExchangeConfig(seed=7)
)
assert rule.information_kind == "exact_fisher"
assert assignment.labels.shape == (normal_scores.shape[0],)
```

The same rows serve either task, and the two tasks are deliberately different. `fit_quantizer`
returns a reusable rule, so `rule.predict_scores(...)` labels events it has never seen.
`optimize_partition` returns an assignment of exactly these rows and stops there — it has no
predict method, because a labelling optimised for one fixed sample is not a rule about future
events.
