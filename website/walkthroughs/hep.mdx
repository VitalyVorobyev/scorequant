# A Higgs search with a floating energy scale

*Simulated collision events are to be reported as a table of {f("bins")} counts. The signal strength is the parameter of interest; the tau energy scale is a nuisance that floats in the fit. Which {f("bins")} categories keep the most information about the signal strength once the energy scale is free to move?*

## 1. The measurement

A search for the Higgs boson decaying to two tau leptons counts collision events that look like the signal and compares the count with the prediction. The quantity reported is the **signal strength** $\mu$, the observed signal rate relative to the nominal prediction: $\mu = 1$ means the prediction holds. Two other quantities are estimated at the same time. The background rate $\nu$ scales the events that look like the signal but are not. The **tau energy scale** $\alpha$ is a calibration parameter: if the detector measures every hadronic tau energy a fraction $\alpha - 1$ too high, every energy-dependent quantity of every event shifts, and the shifted background can imitate a change in $\mu$.

This page is a simulation study on a committed fixture: {f("nEvents")} simulated events from the {f("datasetName")}, {f("signalEvents")} of them signal and {f("backgroundEvents")} background, each carrying a Monte Carlo weight. The weights are far from uniform (a background event stands for roughly five hundred times more collisions than a signal event), so the sample is worth about {f("effectiveEvents")} equally weighted events. The fixture holds row-aligned copies of every event with the energy scale shifted to seven values, which is what makes the effect of $\alpha$ measurable. The energy scale is treated as a **free nuisance**: nothing on this page constrains it, neither in the choice of categories nor in the numbers reported, and the closing section says what a physical constraint would change.

Nothing here describes how a published measurement is performed. The background processes are merged into one component, the event selection is held fixed under the shift, and the classifiers are ordinary gradient-boosted trees. The choices are recorded on the <ReferenceLink to="usecases/hep/">reference page</ReferenceLink>.

## 2. Why bin the events

The likelihood of a collision event has no closed form: a simulation generates events, and the analysis learns from them. What gets published is a table of counts in a few categories, one expected yield per category per process, and a likelihood built on that table. The categories are the compression. A category boundary that separates signal from background well is not necessarily one that survives a floating energy scale, because the region where signal concentrates is also the region the energy scale moves background through, and a category that cannot tell the two apart hands part of its signal sensitivity to the nuisance.

So the question is not only "which {f("bins")} categories separate signal from background" but "which {f("bins")} categories keep the signal strength measurable *after* the background rate and the energy scale have been fitted from the same counts".

## 3. Three coordinates per event

ScoreQuant does not take events. It takes, for each event, the sensitivity of the event's log likelihood to each parameter: one column for $\mu$, one for $\nu$, one for $\alpha$. Nobody has that likelihood, so the columns are estimated through density ratios. A signal-versus-background classifier, calibrated and divided by its training proportions, gives the two rate columns in closed form. A second classifier trained to tell an event's energy-scale-shifted-down copy from its shifted-up copy gives the energy-scale column as a central difference. Both classifiers are cross-fitted with one fold per event, so no event is scored by a model that saw it or its shifted copies, and both are trained under the Monte Carlo weights, so the ratios they estimate are those of the physical mixture rather than of the simulated event list. That is the whole score construction; its diagnostics, the leakage trap it avoids and the convergence check on the finite-difference step are on the reference page.

```python
import numpy as np

import scorequant as sq
from examples.hep_classifier import SCHEMA, cross_fitted_table, load_fixture

data = load_fixture()
table = cross_fitted_table(data)

assert table.scores.shape == (data.n_events, 3)
assert SCHEMA.parameters == ("mu_htautau", "nu_background", "tes")
```

Two facts about this score table matter for everything below. It is a **proxy**: every column came from a trained classifier, so the library records the provenance as an estimated ratio and never describes a result in exact-Fisher language. And the energy-scale column is the noisier of the two constructions: two classifiers trained on disjoint events agree on it only at a correlation of {f("tesScoreCorrelation")}, and per-event noise in that column biases every retention quoted below downwards.

## 4. A reusable rule, fitted on half the events

A partition of these {f("nEvents")} rows would label these rows and nothing else. A search needs a **rule**: a function of an event's three coordinates that returns a category, so that a new event, or a shifted copy of an old one, can be categorised. Everything applied below is a rule, and every rule is built on one half of the events and applied to the other.

The events are split once, stratified by label, into two halves of {f("referenceEvents")}. The rule is fitted on the reference half with `fit_quantizer`, under the **profiled** criterion: keep the information about `mu_htautau` that remains after the other two parameters have been profiled out. The evaluation half is passed as a validation sample, which is diagnostic only and never touches the fit.

```python
from examples.hep_classifier.experiment import HEADLINE_BINS, SOLVER_SEED, event_split

halves = event_split(data.is_signal)
reference = table.subset(halves == 0)
evaluation = table.subset(halves == 1)

ds_rule = sq.fit_quantizer(
    reference.sample(),
    validation=evaluation.sample(),
    n_bins=HEADLINE_BINS,
    criterion=sq.ProfiledDOptimality(("mu_htautau",)),
    config=sq.SoftVoronoiConfig(seed=SOLVER_SEED, initializer_restarts=8, max_steps=400),
)
assert ds_rule.information_kind == "supplied_score_surrogate"
```

Two criteria are compared throughout. **Full D-optimality** maximises the determinant of the binned information over all three parameters at once: it treats $\mu$, $\nu$ and $\alpha$ as equally worth resolving. **Profiled $D_s$** maximises the information about $\mu$ alone after $\nu$ and $\alpha$ have been fitted from the same counts, which is the Schur complement of the binned information. The second is the question this measurement asks. For plain D the finite partition can be compiled into its Voronoi rule; for profiled $D_s$ no such bridge exists, so the reusable rule is fitted directly as a soft Voronoi rule and hardened, exactly as on the <a href="/walkthroughs/michelson">Michelson page</a>.

The classifier output is one number, built to separate signal from background. Any binning of it is a set of vertical stripes: it cannot encode a direction it never contained, and the energy-scale direction is precisely that direction. The profiled rule spends its {f("bins")} cells differently. It keeps cells whose mean energy-scale score differs, so that when the energy scale moves events between cells the count table registers the movement as a nuisance change rather than as a signal change.

## 6. Applying the frozen rule

The rule is frozen and applied to the evaluation half, events it has never seen. What a search publishes is the resulting count table: for each category, the expected signal yield and the expected background yield at the nominal energy scale.

```python
labels = ds_rule.predict_scores(evaluation.scores)

signal_yield = np.bincount(
    labels, weights=np.where(evaluation.is_signal, evaluation.weights, 0.0), minlength=HEADLINE_BINS
)
background_yield = np.bincount(
    labels, weights=np.where(~evaluation.is_signal, evaluation.weights, 0.0), minlength=HEADLINE_BINS
)
assert signal_yield.shape == background_yield.shape == (HEADLINE_BINS,)
```

Applying the same rule to the events' shifted copies gives the yields at every committed energy scale, which is the nuisance template the likelihood needs. Those templates, not the score proxy, are what the next section fits.

## 7. What the count table can measure

The downstream question is the expected uncertainty on the signal strength from the count table's own Poisson likelihood, with $\nu$ and $\alpha$ floating. It is computed from the yield templates built as above, with the simulation's actual energy-scale response rather than the classifier proxy, as an Asimov expected uncertainty at $\mu = 1$. The table gives it for every rule, once with the energy scale held fixed and once with it floating.

| Rule, {f("bins")} categories | σ(μ), energy scale fixed | σ(μ), energy scale floating |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Profiled $D_s$ rule | {f("sigmaDsRuleFixed")} | **{f("sigmaDsRule")}** |
| Plain D rule | {f("sigmaDRuleFixed")} | {f("sigmaDRule")} |
| Classifier intervals, max S²/(S+B) | {f("sigmaIntervalsFixed")} | {f("sigmaIntervals")} |
| Classifier logit, equal width | {f("sigmaLogitFixed")} | {f("sigmaLogit")} |
| Classifier quantile bins | {f("sigmaQuantileFixed")} | {f("sigmaQuantile")} |

The uncertainties are relative to the signal strength at this fixture's yields, and the fixture's total weight is a subsample sum rather than the challenge's expected yield, so the absolute values are not a projected sensitivity; the ratios between rows are the result. Read the columns against each other. With the energy scale fixed, every rule does about equally well, and the classifier-output rules are even slightly ahead: they were built for exactly that question. Once the energy scale floats, the classifier-output rules lose between a factor of two and a factor of three, because the counts cannot distinguish a shifted energy scale from a changed signal strength and the fit is forced to trade the two off. The profiled rule loses a few percent. That is the whole scientific lesson of the page, stated in the quantity the measurement reports.

Two caveats belong next to the table. The rules in it were fitted on all {f("nEvents")} events (the label-free ScoreQuant rules and the classifier-output rules alike), because on a held-out half the count table is too small to rank rules whose uncertainties differ by less than about a third; the held-out directions, which show the same ordering with that noise, are on the reference page. And the Monte Carlo statistical uncertainty of the yields themselves, which a real analysis would add, is about forty times the modelled Poisson uncertainty here: with it, the profiled rule's σ(μ) becomes {f("sigmaDsRuleInflated")} against {f("sigmaLogitInflated")} for the logit bins, and the ranking survives.

Those are the numbers a finite-sample study reports, and they are upper references for what a rule can do, not measurements of one.

## 9. The conventional reference

The most familiar analysis of all, a single threshold on the classifier output chosen to maximise $S/\sqrt{B}$ with a count on either side, is not in the charts above because it does not use the same budget: it has {f("thresholdBins")} cells. With the energy scale fixed it measures the signal strength about as well as the others, σ(μ) of {f("sigmaThresholdFixed")}. With three parameters floating it measures nothing: two counts cannot determine three quantities, so the profiled information is not a small number but an unidentified model. A cut-and-count analysis needs the energy scale constrained from elsewhere, which is a statement about the analysis, not a defect of the cut.

## 11. Limits, and how to reproduce this

**The score is a proxy.** No unbinned likelihood exists, so every retention on this page is a fraction of the *estimated* score's information, and the energy-scale column is reproducible only at a correlation of {f("tesScoreCorrelation")} between independently trained classifiers. The downstream table above does not depend on the proxy for its energy-scale response, which is why it is the primary result.

**The energy scale is unconstrained.** Neither the criterion nor any retention number includes an external constraint on $\alpha$. Adding a Gaussian constraint of width {f("tesConstraintWidth")} to the downstream likelihood moves the profiled rule's σ(μ) from {f("sigmaDsRule")} to {f("sigmaDsRuleConstrained")}: the yields self-constrain the energy scale far below that width, so a physical constraint changes nothing here.

**The fixture is small.** {f("nEvents")} events, one split, and held-out cells with as few as {f("dsRuleMinBinEvents")} simulated events. The held-out envelopes are wide for that reason, and the two directions of the split are reported separately rather than only as a mean. A larger fixture is a data decision, not a code change.
