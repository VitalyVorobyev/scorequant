# Phase Estimation in a Michelson Interferometer

*A continuous measurement with an analytically known score is to be reported through a fixed number of bins $K$. One parameter is of interest and the others are nuisances. The bins should preserve as much Fisher information as possible.*

## 1. The Michelson interferometer

A [Michelson interferometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelson_interferometer) splits a coherent beam of light into two perpendicular arms. Each beam is reflected by a mirror, returns to the beam splitter, and the two beams are recombined on a detector. Their relative phase is set by the difference in optical path length between the arms.

<MichelsonBench
  caption="Schematic optical layout. The segmented detector shows the equal-width baseline."
/>

The beams have a finite transverse width, so they overlap over an area rather than at a point. If the two wavefronts are slightly tilted with respect to each other, the relative phase varies with position across the detector, and the intensity alternates between bright and dark bands. These are **interference fringes**, or simply **fringes**.

For a nearly linear phase variation along one detector coordinate $u$,

$$
I(u)\propto 1+V\cos(ku+\varphi),
$$

where $V$ is the fringe visibility (the contrast of the pattern), $k$ is the spatial fringe frequency, and $\varphi$ is the phase offset. A change in the optical path difference shifts $\varphi$ and moves the whole pattern across the detector. The tilt is only what produces spatial fringes; it is not a parameter of the statistical model below.

[Albert A. Michelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_A._Michelson) introduced the instrument in the late nineteenth century. Gravitational-wave detectors such as [LIGO](https://www.ligo.caltech.edu/page/what-is-interferometer) use the same interference principle with far more elaborate optics.

## 2. Why bin the measurement?

A real interferometric detector is not the ideal model above. Illumination, detector efficiency, fringe visibility, background, optical aberrations and calibration errors all vary with position. Describing them at full detector resolution can require a large nuisance model that is hard to validate.

Binning replaces point-by-point response modelling with the integrated response of a small number of regions. The nuisance model then has a few calibrated quantities instead of one per pixel.

Once the measurement is described by $K$ bins, the bins still have to be defined. Different partitions preserve different amounts of information.

## 3. Measurement model

The detector is described by a dimensionless coordinate $u$, scaled so that one fringe period has length $2\pi$. The detector spans {f("fringes")} fringes, so

$$
u\in[0,8\pi).
$$

The position of one detected photon has density

$$
p(u\mid\varphi,\epsilon)
\propto
1+V\cos\!\bigl((1+\epsilon)u+\varphi\bigr).
$$

Here $\varphi$ is the phase, ultimately the parameter of interest. The visibility is fixed at $V={}${f("visibility")}. The parameter $\epsilon$ is a small fractional error in the spatial fringe frequency: it changes the fringe spacing across the detector, so an unknown $\epsilon$ can partly imitate a phase shift.

All scores and information matrices are evaluated at the reference point

$$
(\varphi_0,\epsilon_0)=(0,0).
$$

## 4. The analytic score

ScoreQuant works with the score of each observation, the gradient of the log density with respect to the parameters. Here it is known in closed form. At the reference point,

$$
s_\varphi(u)
=
-\frac{V\sin u}{1+V\cos u},
\qquad
s_\epsilon(u)
=
u\,s_\varphi(u)-V.
$$

The phase score is largest in magnitude on the slopes of a fringe, where a small phase shift changes the intensity most, and vanishes at the bright and dark extrema. The frequency score is the same local sensitivity multiplied by $u$, because a frequency error accumulates across the detector. Its constant term $-V$ is the derivative of the normalization of the density with respect to $\epsilon$; without it the score would not have zero mean and the Fisher matrix would be wrong.

The score is supplied to ScoreQuant as a function of the observation. Fixed model constants are uppercase; computed arrays are lowercase.

```python
import numpy as np
import scorequant as sq

EXECUTION = sq.ExecutionConfig(backend="numpy", precision="float64", device="cpu")

VISIBILITY = 0.60
N_FRINGES = 4
U_MAX = 2.0 * np.pi * N_FRINGES

SCHEMA = sq.ScoreSchema(("phase", "fringe_frequency"))
INTEREST = SCHEMA.select("phase")


def fringe_density(observations):
    """Normalized photon density along the detector at the reference point."""
    u = np.asarray(observations)[:, 0]
    return (1.0 + VISIBILITY * np.cos(u)) / U_MAX


def michelson_score(observations):
    """Score (s_phase, s_frequency) at the reference point, in closed form."""
    u = np.asarray(observations)[:, 0]
    s_phase = -VISIBILITY * np.sin(u) / (1.0 + VISIBILITY * np.cos(u))
    return np.column_stack([s_phase, u * s_phase - VISIBILITY])


provider = sq.ScoreFunction(
    michelson_score,
    provenance=sq.ScoreProvenance(kind="exact", reference_point=(0.0, 0.0)),
    schema=SCHEMA,
)
```

For the finite optimization the detector is represented by a fine deterministic grid. Each node carries the probability mass of its small interval.

```python
N_NODES = 8_000
step = U_MAX / N_NODES

u = (np.arange(N_NODES) + 0.5) * step
observations = u[:, None]
weights = fringe_density(observations) * step
scores = provider.score(observations, execution=EXECUTION)
```

The arrays `scores` and `weights` form the weighted score table used by every finite partition below.

## 5. Quantization in score space

The score maps every detector position to a point

$$
u \longmapsto s(u)=\bigl(s_\varphi(u),\,s_\epsilon(u)\bigr)
$$

in a two-dimensional score space. The image is a one-dimensional trajectory: one loop per fringe, each loop taller than the last because the frequency score grows with $u$.

<Figure
  src={siteUrl("figures/michelson-score-space.svg")}
  alt="The Michelson model in score space: the phase score on the horizontal axis against the fringe-frequency score on the vertical, drawn as four narrow loops radiating from a common point near the origin, each successive loop longer and more steeply inclined."
  caption={<>The detector mapped into score space. Each point of the curve is one position <Tex>{"u"}</Tex>.</>}
  paper
/>

A partition into $K$ cells with probability masses $W_b$ and mean scores $\mu_b$ retains the Fisher information

$$
I_{\mathrm{bin}}
=
\sum_{b=1}^{K} W_b\,\mu_b\mu_b^\top ,
$$

a two-by-two matrix here. The first question is how to divide score space into $K$ cells if information about both parameters is to be preserved jointly. The standard answer is **D-optimality**:

$$
\max_{q}\ \log\det I_{\mathrm{bin}} .
$$

The determinant is the volume of information across all directions of score space, so maximizing it preserves information in every direction at once rather than favouring one.

## 6. D-optimal partition

With $K$ fixed, `optimize_partition` assigns the weighted score table to $K$ cells by exact single-point exchange: a node is moved between cells whenever the move increases the determinant, until no single move can.

```python
N_BINS = 6

d_partition = sq.optimize_partition(
    scores,
    weights=weights,
    n_bins=N_BINS,
    criterion=sq.DOptimality(),
    config=sq.DExchangeConfig(seed=4),
    execution=EXECUTION,
)
d_labels = d_partition.labels
```

In score space the cells are regular connected regions with straight edges. Along the detector the same labels form a comb of {f("dRunsAtSix")} separate intervals from {f("bins")} cells. There is no contradiction. The trajectory folds back on itself once per fringe, so a connected cell in score space meets it several times. The four outer cells each own the tips of a pair of loops; the two cells at the origin, where every loop begins and ends, are crossed twice per loop and pull back to the most intervals. A short detector interval appears wherever the trajectory only briefly crosses a cell: {f("dNarrowRuns")} of the intervals are narrower than {f("narrowRunWidth")} in $u$, the narrowest {f("dMinRunWidth")}.

This is the geometrically clean case. An exchange-stable D-optimal partition with a nonsingular Fisher matrix is exactly a Mahalanobis–Voronoi partition of score space, with the inverse of the binned Fisher matrix as metric. The theorem and its proof are in <ReferenceLink to="book/ch08-d-optimality/">D-optimality and exact exchange</ReferenceLink>; the shading in the figure is that rule evaluated on a grid.

## 7. A reusable D-optimal readout

The finite partition labels the {f("nNodes")} nodes of the table and nothing else: `PartitionResult` deliberately has no predict method. Because of the theorem above, the D-optimal result can be compiled into the Voronoi rule it already is. That rule assigns any score vector, so any new observation.

```python
d_quantizer = d_partition.compile_quantizer(execution=EXECUTION)

new_observations = np.array([[1.0], [7.0], [15.0]])
new_scores = provider.score(new_observations, execution=EXECUTION)
new_bins_d = d_quantizer.predict_scores(new_scores, execution=EXECUTION)
```

The deployment path is always explicit:

$$
u
\;\longrightarrow\;
s(u)
\;\longrightarrow\;
q\!\left(s(u)\right).
$$

The observation-to-score step is never hidden inside prediction. For the three positions above the compiled rule returns bins {f("dPredictionOne")}, {f("dPredictionTwo")} and {f("dPredictionThree")}, and on the training table it reproduces every one of the {f("nNodes")} finite labels.

Two objects, two tasks. `optimize_partition` labels a fixed weighted table. A quantizer is an inductive rule on score space. The compile bridge connects them only for exchange-stable D-optimal partitions; the general route to a quantizer is `fit_quantizer`, used for the profiled readout below. The distinction is spelled out in <ReferenceLink to="book/ch06-two-tasks/">Two tasks and three optimization levels</ReferenceLink>.

## 8. Treating fringe frequency as a nuisance

The scientific question now changes. The phase $\varphi$ is the result to be reported. The frequency error $\epsilon$ is estimated only because uncertainty in it can imitate a phase shift.

Partition the binned Fisher matrix,

$$
I_{\mathrm{bin}}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
I_{\varphi\varphi} & I_{\varphi\epsilon}\\
I_{\varphi\epsilon} & I_{\epsilon\epsilon}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The information about $\varphi$ that survives estimating $\epsilon$ from the same data is the **profiled phase information**

$$
I_{\varphi\mid\epsilon}
=
I_{\varphi\varphi}
-
\frac{I_{\varphi\epsilon}^2}{I_{\epsilon\epsilon}}.
$$

The subtracted term is the part of the phase sensitivity that a change in $\epsilon$ can reproduce. In this model the two scores are strongly correlated, {f("correlation")} at the reference point, and profiling removes {f("costOfProfiling")} of the unbinned phase information before any binning.

Ordinary D-optimality treats $\varphi$ and $\epsilon$ symmetrically: it spends cells on resolving $\epsilon$ as carefully as $\varphi$. The **profiled criterion** $D_s$ asks instead how the $K$ cells should be spent if only $\varphi$ will be reported,

$$
\max_{q}\ \log I_{\varphi\mid\epsilon}^{\mathrm{bin}} .
$$

## 9. Optimizing for phase

Quality is measured by the fraction of the unbinned profiled phase information that a partition retains,

$$
R_\varphi
=
\frac{I_{\varphi\mid\epsilon}^{\mathrm{bin}}}
     {I_{\varphi\mid\epsilon}^{\mathrm{full}}},
$$

with the profiled quantity in both numerator and denominator. `profiled_information_report` computes exactly that ratio for any labeling.

```python
def phase_retention(labels):
    """Profiled phase information retained, as a fraction of the unbinned value."""
    report = sq.profiled_information_report(
        scores, labels, interest=INTEREST, weights=weights, n_bins=N_BINS, execution=EXECUTION
    )
    return report.geometric_mean_retention


equal_labels = np.digitize(u, np.linspace(0.0, U_MAX, N_BINS + 1)[1:-1])
equal_width_retained = phase_retention(equal_labels)
d_retained = phase_retention(d_labels)
```

For one parameter of interest ScoreQuant can also bound the best possible profiled objective at a given $K$: the efficient score $\hat s = s_\varphi - (I_{\varphi\epsilon}/I_{\epsilon\epsilon})\,s_\epsilon$ is scalar, the profiled information of any hard rule is dominated by the between-cell information of $\hat s$, and the best interval partition of a scalar is found exactly by dynamic programming. The result is a certified ceiling, and its interval labels are a good starting point for the exchange solver.

```python
bound = sq.efficient_score_bound(
    scores, interest=INTEREST, weights=weights, n_bins=N_BINS, execution=EXECUTION
)

ds_partition = sq.optimize_partition(
    scores,
    weights=weights,
    n_bins=N_BINS,
    criterion=sq.ProfiledDOptimality(interest=INTEREST),
    config=sq.DExchangeConfig(seed=4),
    initial_labels=bound.labels,
    execution=EXECUTION,
)
ds_labels = ds_partition.labels
ds_retained = phase_retention(ds_labels)
```

The profiled partition cuts across the loops instead of around them. The cells no longer separate the loops from one another, which is what resolving $\epsilon$ required; they separate values of the efficient score, the part of the phase sensitivity that the nuisance cannot reproduce.

The finite profiled optimum does not, in general, obey the Mahalanobis–Voronoi geometry of the ordinary D case: a stable profiled partition can violate the nearest-cell rule of the very metric it induces, and even a global optimum can. This is why the top panel has no shaded cells and why the result refuses `compile_quantizer`. The counterexample is worked out in <ReferenceLink to="examples/ds-geometry-counterexample/">A profiled optimum outside its geometry</ReferenceLink>.

Along the detector the profiled partition is a comb of {f("dsRunsAtSix")} intervals, the same count as the D comb, but the pieces fall differently: {f("dsNarrowRuns")} intervals are narrower than {f("narrowRunWidth")} in $u$, against {f("dNarrowRuns")} for D, and the narrowest is {f("dsMinRunWidth")} wide and carries a probability mass of {f("dsMinRunMass")}. They sit at the dark fringes, where $\hat s$ crosses zero steeply, so a short stretch of detector covers a long stretch of efficient score. The middle panel shows the same slicing on the curve itself.

Where the fragments come from is a question the run can answer. The certified initializer is an interval partition of $\hat s$, and pulled back to the detector it already has {f("dsInitialNarrowRuns")} fragments among its {f("dsInitialRuns")} intervals, retaining {f("dsInitialRetention")}. The exchange then relabels {f("dsRelabelledFraction")} of the nodes in {f("dsAcceptedMoves")} accepted moves over {f("dsScans")} scans, gains {f("dsRetentionGain")} in retention, and leaves the number of intervals and their distribution over the cells unchanged. The fragmentation is the shape of the interval optimum in the efficient score, not an artefact of the exchange.

What the fragments are worth is a second question. Absorbing every interval narrower than {f("smoothingOneMinWidth")} into its wider neighbour leaves {f("smoothingOneRuns")} intervals and retains {f("smoothingOneRetention")}; at {f("smoothingTwoMinWidth")} the figures are {f("smoothingTwoRuns")} and {f("smoothingTwoRetention")}; at {f("smoothingThreeMinWidth")}, {f("smoothingThreeRuns")} and {f("smoothingThreeRetention")}; at {f("smoothingFourMinWidth")}, {f("smoothingFourRuns")} intervals still using all {f("smoothingFourBinsUsed")} bins and {f("smoothingFourRetention")}. The ladder is monotone. Every fragment removed costs retention, the cost grows with the width absorbed, and even the narrowest pieces are not free. The fragments are part of the optimum rather than noise, so no smoothing or regularization is applied to the committed partition. A simpler detector geometry is bought with retention; the next section shows what a rule with that simpler geometry retains.

## 11. Reusable profiled quantizer

The finite profiled labels have no canonical rule to compile into. A deployable profiled readout is therefore fitted directly as a rule, with `fit_quantizer`, from a reference measure and the same score function. The measure is the continuous detector density, integrated by Gauss–Legendre quadrature.

```python
source = sq.IntegrationSource(
    [[0.0, U_MAX]],
    density=fringe_density,
    quadrature=sq.GaussLegendreConfig(order=256),
)
```

`SoftVoronoiConfig` optimizes the positions of $K$ centres in whitened score space with smooth assignments, then hardens them into a nearest-centre rule.

```python
ds_quantizer = sq.fit_quantizer(
    source,
    provider=provider,
    n_bins=N_BINS,
    criterion=sq.ProfiledDOptimality(interest=INTEREST),
    config=sq.SoftVoronoiConfig(seed=4, initializer_restarts=8, max_steps=1000),
    execution=EXECUTION,
)

new_bins_ds = ds_quantizer.predict_scores(new_scores, execution=EXECUTION)
```

For the three new positions the profiled rule returns bins {f("dsRulePredictionOne")}, {f("dsRulePredictionTwo")} and {f("dsRulePredictionThree")}. Applied to the training table, it retains {f("dsRuleProfiledRetention")} of the profiled phase information against {f("profiledRetentionAtSix")} for the finite partition, and pulls back to {f("dsRuleRuns")} detector intervals against {f("dsRunsAtSix")}. Its hardening gap, the change in retention from the soft objective to the hard rule, is {f("dsRuleHardeningGap")}.

A nearest-centre rule is a restricted family. The finite optimum is not in it, so the reusable rule retains less; in exchange it is a rule, with a simpler detector geometry, that classifies any observation. Which of the two matters depends on whether the readout must be deployed or only studied.

## 13. Summary

1. The score turns the detector coordinate into the space that matters for inference: a folded trajectory in a two-dimensional score space.
2. D-optimality gives a clean information-preserving quantizer when every parameter matters. Its finite partition is a Mahalanobis–Voronoi partition and compiles into a reusable rule; along the detector it is a comb.
3. Profiling changes the objective when only a subset of parameters will be reported. The cells then separate values of the efficient score rather than resolving the nuisance.
4. For this instrument at {f("bins")} bins, optimizing for phase raises the retained profiled phase information from {f("dOptimalRetentionAtSix")} to {f("profiledRetentionAtSix")}, against a certified ceiling of {f("ceilingRetentionAtSix")}.
5. Partitioning a fixed table and fitting a reusable quantizer are related but distinct tasks. The D partition compiles into a quantizer; the profiled partition does not, and its deployable counterpart is fitted as a rule.
