# Phase Estimation in a Michelson Interferometer

*Given a continuous measurement, an analytically known score for the parameter of interest and nuisance parameters, and a fixed number of bins $K$, find a partition that preserves as much profiled Fisher information about the parameter of interest as possible.*

## 1. The Michelson interferometer

A [Michelson interferometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelson_interferometer) splits a coherent beam of light into two perpendicular paths. Each beam is reflected by a mirror and returns to the beam splitter, where the two beams are recombined and directed toward a detector. Their relative phase depends on the difference in optical path length between the two arms.

<MichelsonBench
  caption="Schematic optical layout. The segmented detector illustrates the equal-width baseline"
/>

The beams have a finite transverse width, so after recombination they overlap over an area rather than at a single point. If their wavefronts are perfectly aligned, the relative phase is approximately constant across that area. If the wavefronts are slightly tilted relative to one another, the phase changes with position on the detector. The resulting alternating bright and dark bands are called **interference fringes**, or simply **fringes**.

For a nearly linear phase variation along one detector coordinate $u$, the intensity can be written in the simple form

$$
I(u)\propto 1+V\cos(ku+\varphi),
$$

where $V$ is the fringe visibility, $k$ is the spatial fringe frequency, and $\varphi$ is the phase offset. Changing the optical path difference shifts $\varphi$, and therefore shifts the fringe pattern across the detector.

The interferometer was introduced by [Albert A. Michelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_A._Michelson) in the late nineteenth century and became a fundamental instrument for precision optical measurements. Modern gravitational-wave detectors such as [LIGO](https://www.ligo.caltech.edu/page/what-is-interferometer) use the same basic interference principle, although with much more elaborate optical and control systems.

Here we use this simple one-dimensional fringe model as a controlled setting for studying information-preserving quantization.

## 2. Why bin the measurement?

A realistic interferometric measurement is more complicated than the ideal fringe model above. The observed pattern can be affected by spatial variations in illumination, detector efficiency, fringe visibility, background, optical aberrations, and calibration errors. Describing all of these effects at the full spatial resolution of the detector may require a large and difficult-to-validate nuisance model.

Binning provides a controlled reduction of this complexity. Instead of modeling the detector response point by point, the analysis works with integrated response over a finite set of regions. The corresponding nuisance model can then be expressed in terms of a much smaller number of quantities that can be calibrated and profiled.

Once we choose to describe the measurement with $K$ bins, we still have to decide how to define them. Different choices preserve different amounts of information about the parameter of interest. Our goal is to find the partition that preserves the most Fisher information after profiling the nuisance parameters.

In the model below, the phase $\varphi$ is the parameter of interest, while an uncertainty in the spatial fringe frequency, $\epsilon$, serves as a simple nuisance parameter.

## 3. Measurement model

We describe the detector by a one-dimensional coordinate $u$, scaled so that one fringe period has length $2\pi$. In this example the detector spans four complete fringes, so

$$
u\in[0,8\pi).
$$

The position of a detected photon is modeled by

$$
p(u\mid\varphi,\epsilon)
\propto
1+V\cos\!\bigl((1+\epsilon)u+\varphi\bigr).
$$

The phase $\varphi$ is the parameter we want to measure. The visibility $V$ controls the contrast of the interference fringes: $V=1$ gives maximum contrast, while $V=0$ removes the fringe pattern entirely. We fix $V=0.60$.

The parameter $\epsilon$ describes a small fractional uncertainty in the spatial fringe frequency. It changes the fringe spacing across the detector and can therefore partially mimic a phase shift. We treat $\epsilon$ as a nuisance parameter.

All information calculations below are evaluated at the nominal model,

$$
(\varphi_0,\epsilon_0)=(0,0).
$$

## 4. The analytic score

ScoreQuant works with the score of each observation. In this example the score is known analytically and can be computed directly from the interference model.

At the nominal point $(\varphi,\epsilon)=(0,0)$, the two score components are

$$
s_\varphi(u)
=
\frac{-V\sin u}{1+V\cos u},
$$

and

$$
s_\epsilon(u)
=
u\,s_\varphi(u)-V.
$$

The phase score $s_\varphi$ is largest in magnitude on the slopes of a fringe, where a small phase shift produces the largest relative change in intensity. It vanishes at the bright and dark extrema, where the intensity is locally insensitive to a shift.

The frequency score $s_\epsilon$ contains the same local phase sensitivity multiplied by the position $u$. This reflects the fact that a small frequency error accumulates across the detector: its effect is small near $u=0$ and grows with distance. The constant term $-V$ comes from the normalization of the probability density and ensures that the score has zero expectation.

Each detector position is thus represented by a two-dimensional score vector,

$$
s(u)=\bigl(s_\varphi(u),\,s_\epsilon(u)\bigr).
$$

Because the score is available analytically, we provide these functions directly to `ScoreQuant`.

```python
import numpy as np
import scorequant as sq

EXECUTION = sq.ExecutionConfig(backend="numpy", precision="float64", device="cpu")

VISIBILITY = 0.6
FRINGES = 4
U_MAX = 2.0 * np.pi * FRINGES

SCHEMA = sq.ScoreSchema(("phase", "fringe_frequency"))
INTEREST = SCHEMA.select("phase")


def fringe_density(observations):
    """The normalized one-photon arrival law along the fringe coordinate."""
    u = np.asarray(observations)[:, 0]
    return (1.0 + VISIBILITY * np.cos(u)) / U_MAX


def michelson_score(observations):
    """Sensitivity to (phase, fringe_frequency) at the reference point, in closed form."""
    u = np.asarray(observations)[:, 0]
    s_phase = -VISIBILITY * np.sin(u) / (1.0 + VISIBILITY * np.cos(u))
    return np.column_stack([s_phase, u * s_phase - VISIBILITY])


provider = sq.ScoreFunction(
    michelson_score,
    provenance=sq.ScoreProvenance(kind="exact", reference_point=(0.0, 0.0)),
    schema=SCHEMA,
)
```

For the numerical optimization we represent the detector by a fine deterministic grid. Each grid point carries the probability mass of its small detector interval.

```python
N_NODES = 8_000
step = U_MAX / N_NODES

u = (np.arange(N_NODES) + 0.5) * step
observations = u[:, None]

weights = fringe_density(observations) * step
scores = np.asarray(
    provider.score(observations, execution=EXECUTION)
)
```

The resulting arrays `scores` and `weights` form the finite weighted score table used throughout the rest of the example.

## 5. Quantizing the score space

The analytic score maps every detector position $u$ to a point

$$
u \longmapsto s(u)=\bigl(s_\varphi(u),s_\epsilon(u)\bigr)
$$

in a two-dimensional score space. Our goal is to divide this score distribution into $K$ bins while retaining as much profiled phase information as possible.

Numerically, we approximate the continuous detector coordinate by the fine weighted table constructed above. The optimization therefore assigns the $N_{\mathrm{nodes}}$ weighted score points to $K$ bins. With a sufficiently fine grid, this finite partition provides a numerical approximation to the continuous quantization problem.

  caption="The detector mapped into score space. Each point on the curve corresponds to one position $u$ on the detector."

For a partition with cell probabilities $W_b$ and mean scores $\mu_b$, the Fisher information retained by the $K$ bins is

$$
I_{\mathrm{bin}}
=
\sum_{b=1}^{K} W_b\,\mu_b\mu_b^\top .
$$

In our two-parameter problem,

$$
I_{\mathrm{bin}}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
I_{\varphi\varphi} & I_{\varphi\epsilon}\\
I_{\varphi\epsilon} & I_{\epsilon\epsilon}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Because $\epsilon$ is a nuisance parameter, the information relevant for measuring $\varphi$ is the **profiled Fisher information**

$$
I_{\varphi\mid\epsilon}
=
I_{\varphi\varphi}
-
\frac{I_{\varphi\epsilon}^2}{I_{\epsilon\epsilon}}.
$$

The second term is the information lost because a change in $\epsilon$ can imitate part of a phase shift. Our optimization problem is therefore simple: among all partitions into $K$ cells, find the one that maximizes $I_{\varphi\mid\epsilon}$.

In ScoreQuant this is the $D_s$-optimal criterion, with $\varphi$ selected as the parameter of interest.

## 6. Optimizing the finite partition

For the main example we use $K=6$ bins. We optimize the assignment of the weighted score table to these bins using the profiled $D_s$ criterion, with $\varphi$ as the parameter of interest and $\epsilon$ as the nuisance parameter.

Before running the optimizer, we compute an efficient-score bound. For this finite weighted problem, it provides an upper bound on the profiled information attainable with $K$ bins and also supplies an initial partition.

```python
N_BINS = 6

bound = sq.efficient_score_bound(
    scores,
    interest=INTEREST,
    weights=weights,
    n_bins=N_BINS,
    execution=EXECUTION,
)

result = sq.optimize_partition(
    scores,
    weights=weights,
    n_bins=N_BINS,
    criterion=sq.ProfiledDOptimality(interest=INTEREST),
    config=sq.DExchangeConfig(seed=4),
    initial_labels=bound.labels,
    execution=EXECUTION,
)
```

`DExchangeConfig` uses single-point exchanges: detector positions are moved between bins whenever the move increases the objective. The optimization stops when no further single-point improvement is possible.

The resulting `result.labels` assigns one of the six bins to every position in the quadrature table. The next step is to compare the information retained by this partition with simpler alternatives.

## 7. Results

We measure the quality of a partition by the fraction of the original profiled phase information that it retains,

$$
R_\varphi
=
\frac{I_{\varphi\mid\epsilon}^{\mathrm{bin}}}
     {I_{\varphi\mid\epsilon}^{\mathrm{full}}}.
$$

Equal-width binning retains only about 21% of the available phase information. Optimizing the full $D$ criterion performs much better, but its objective is to preserve information about $\varphi$ and $\epsilon$ jointly. Optimizing $D_s$ directly for the phase retains about 95%.

The $D_s$ result reaches the certified upper bound to the precision shown. Thus, for this $K=6$ weighted score table, no other six-bin partition can retain more profiled phase information.

## 8. Interpreting the partition

The optimized bins are clearly not six contiguous segments of the detector. The same label appears in several separated regions of $u$.

This is a natural consequence of optimizing in score space. A bin represents a group of observations with a similar role in the inference, not necessarily a connected region of the original measurement coordinate. Positions that are far apart on the detector can therefore be assigned to the same bin.

Mapped back to $u$, the result has a comb-like structure across the fringe pattern. Equal-width binning cannot reproduce this structure because it groups observations only by spatial proximity.

This is the main qualitative result of the example: **information-optimal bins need not resemble a conventional geometric segmentation of the measurement space.**

## 9. From a finite partition to a quantizer

So far we have optimized a finite table of $N_{\mathrm{nodes}}$ quadrature points. The resulting `result.labels` tells us how to classify those particular points, but a practical quantizer must also assign a bin to a new observation.

For ordinary $D$-optimality, an exchange-stable finite partition can be converted into a Mahalanobis–Voronoi rule. For profiled $D_s$, this is not true in general: a finite optimum does not necessarily define a canonical nearest-cell rule. The reason is discussed in more detail in [Nuisance parameters and profiled $D_s$](pathname:///reference/book/ch10-profiled-ds/).

Instead, we fit a reusable profiled quantizer directly. We represent the continuous detector distribution with an integration source,

```python
source = sq.IntegrationSource(
    [[0.0, U_MAX]],
    density=fringe_density,
    quadrature=sq.GaussLegendreConfig(order=256),
)
```

and optimize a rule in score space:

```python
quantizer = sq.fit_quantizer(
    source,
    provider=provider,
    n_bins=N_BINS,
    criterion=sq.ProfiledDOptimality(interest=INTEREST),
    config=sq.SoftVoronoiConfig(
        seed=4,
        initializer_restarts=8,
        max_steps=300,
    ),
    execution=EXECUTION,
)
```

During optimization, `SoftVoronoiConfig` uses smooth assignments so that the cell positions can be optimized continuously. The final result is hardened into a discrete $K$-bin rule.

The complete mapping for a new detector observation is then

$$
u
\;\longrightarrow\;
s(u)
\;\longrightarrow\;
q\!\left(s(u)\right).
$$

In code:

```python
new_observations = np.array([[1.0], [7.0], [15.0]])

new_scores = provider.score(
    new_observations,
    execution=EXECUTION,
)

new_bins = quantizer.predict_scores(
    new_scores,
    execution=EXECUTION,
)
```

Unlike the finite `result.labels`, this rule can be applied to arbitrary new observations.

The finite partition and the fitted quantizer are therefore two related but distinct objects. The first provides a direct numerical study of the information-optimal assignment on a fine representation of the measurement. The second provides the inductive rule needed for actual use.

## 10. What we learned

This example started from a continuous interferometric measurement with one parameter of interest, one nuisance parameter, and an analytically known score. With only six bins, equal-width segmentation retains about 21% of the available profiled phase information, while an optimized profiled partition retains about 95%.

The improvement does not come from placing six better boundaries along the detector. ScoreQuant groups observations according to the information they carry about the parameters, producing non-contiguous bins when that is advantageous.

The workflow is general:

$$
\text{measurement model}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{score}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{information objective}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{optimized quantization}.
$$

The Michelson model makes every step explicit enough to see what is happening. In more complicated applications the score may come from a numerical model or an estimator rather than an analytic formula, but the quantization problem is the same.
